How it works

Why coastline shape decides the game

Standard Catan is a fixed hexagon. This tool builds free-form "pangaea" continents instead — but freehand shapes create far more coastline than the official board, and coastline quietly wrecks resource balance. Here's the actual mechanism, and what every control on the left does about it.

The core problem

Every point where tiles meet — an intersection, where a settlement can go — touches either 1, 2, or 3 tiles. A 3-tile spot is the good one: it collects three resources' worth of dice rolls every turn. Reshape the same tiles into a spikier outline and the tile count doesn't change, but 3-spots get converted into 1- and 2-spots.

The terms

Anumber of land tiles
Snumber of land-to-land tile edges — how many pairs of tiles touch
v1 / v2 / v3count of 1-, 2-, 3-resource intersections
Smaxthe highest S possible for A tiles — a perfectly round blob

These counts are locked together by simple counting — a consequence of Euler's formula, the rule that says any shape made of flat faces meeting at edges and corners always balances out a certain way (the same idea behind why a soccer ball's pentagons and hexagons always add up correctly):

v3 = S − A + 1 − (enclosed lakes) v2 = 3A − S − 3 v1 = 3A − S + 3

v3 depends only on S. Shape matters only through how many tile edges it creates. The maximum is Smax = ⌊3A − √(12A − 3)⌋; every edge short of that destroys one 3-spot and manufactures one 1-spot and one 2-spot. That relationship is the entire problem, in one line.

What the engine actually does

1
Compactify — hill-climb tile positions to raise S toward a target floor.
2
Place ports — walk the coastline, space them evenly, site resource-specific ports near (not on top of) that resource.
3
Anneal terrain + numbers — simulated annealing against a scoring function (below).
4
Score — simulate a real snake draft across your table size and report the result.

What "simulated annealing" means

Annealing is a metallurgy term: heat metal, then cool it slowly so atoms settle into a stable, low-energy arrangement instead of freezing wherever they happened to be. The algorithm borrows the idea — start with terrain/numbers randomly placed, then repeatedly try a random swap. A swap that makes the board better is always kept. A swap that makes it worse is still sometimes kept, with a probability that shrinks as a "temperature" value cools over the run.

That last rule is the whole point. A plain hill-climb (only ever accept improvements) gets stuck the moment every single swap looks worse, even if a genuinely good board is two swaps away. Annealing tolerates bad moves early and only tightens up near the end — which is why it needs thousands of passes rather than one clever calculation.

What the score actually penalizes

Every candidate board gets a penalty score, lower is better: a 6 touching an 8 (or another 6/8) is penalized heavily, as are duplicate adjacent numbers and same-resource clumping. Resource pips are checked against tile count, not forced equal — ore and brick legitimately have fewer tiles, so fewer pips is correct, not a flaw. And critically: the engine actually simulates a snake draft (players greedily grabbing the best open spot) and penalizes any board where the best and worst seat end up far apart.

The code

One self-contained file — hex-grid math, then topology/counting, continent growth, ports and sea-lanes, the scoring function, the annealer, the SVG renderer, the analysis panel, and the draft mode's turn-based state machine, in that order. No build step, no dependencies — it runs entirely in the browser.
